## How to Plan a Manga Page Before Using an AI Manga Generator

Every good manga page starts with a plan, and that's also true when you're figuring out how to make manga with AI. Mapping out the scene first saves you from wasting generations on pages that miss the point, and it gives you a blueprint to follow when you write the prompt in PixAI.

Your plan should cover the basics, like how many characters speak, where the scene takes place, how many panels the page needs, and whether it's in color or black and white. For our test, we chose a full-color page with two characters in a medieval throne room. We split it into four panels in a 2×2 grid that reads left to right and top to bottom.

From there, each panel gets its own shot, action, and line of dialogue. Panel 1 is a wider shot of the knight standing tall with one hand on his sword hilt as the king listens from his throne, and he declares, "Your Majesty, I've come to slay the legendary White Dragon!" Panel 2 moves to a medium close-up of the king, who calmly replies, "The dragon hasn't been seen for months."

*The Process of Planning a Manga Page, Beat By Beat*

The bottom row tightens the framing. Panel 3 is a close-up of the knight tilting his head with a self-assured grin as he says, "That may be, but I'll still take a look." Panel 4 closes in on the king, who rests his cheek on one hand and answers with a mildly annoyed "Good luck."

Each panel does a different job, moving from a bold claim to doubt, then stubbornness, then a dry reply. Varying the framing and emotion keeps the page from feeling like the same pose repeated four times, and it helps an AI manga panel generator understand what each panel is for.

We later changed the knight's opening line to "My King," which we'll cover in the dialogue section.

## Write a Manga Page Prompt for an AI Manga Page Generator

Next, we turned the plan into a prompt for Tsubaki.3. Rather than writing one long description of the scene, we split it into page-level instructions that apply to the whole page and panel-level instructions that tell the model what happens in each frame. If you're new to the model, PixAI's [Tsubaki.3 prompt guide](https://blog.pixai.art/en/tsubaki-3-prompt-guide/) covers its prompting basics in more detail.

Here's a closer look at how our prompt was organized:

- **Overall Look:** The opening lines set a modern anime style, full-color artwork, clean linework, white speech bubbles, and a throne room setting, along with a request to keep both characters consistent across all four panels.
- **Grid and Spacing:** A second paragraph places the panels in an evenly spaced 2×2 grid, with Panels 1 and 2 on the top row and Panels 3 and 4 below.
- **Panel-by-Panel Beats:** Each of the four panels gets its own shot, action, expression, and a speech bubble assigned to a specific speaker.
- **Character Appearance:** We left out descriptions of the knight and king because we supplied reference images instead, which you'll see in the next section.

Assigning every line to a speaker means Tsubaki.3 doesn't have to guess who's talking. Looking back, there are two things we'd tighten. Our prompt implies the reading order through panel positions but never states it, so adding a line like "Read left to right, top to bottom" makes it explicit. We also asked for consistent designs only in general terms instead of naming key details like the king's crown, and that came back to bite us in the first generation.

Here's the full prompt we used.

```text
Create a 4-panel manga page in modern anime style, full-color manga artwork with clean linework, detailed backgrounds, expressive facial expressions, and clear white speech bubbles. The scene takes place in a grand medieval throne room with tall stone pillars, ornate banners, and a majestic throne. Keep the knight and king's character designs, outfits, and appearances consistent across all four panels.

Arrange the 4 manga panels in a clean, evenly spaced 2×2 grid. Top row: Panel 1 on the left, Panel 2 on the right. Bottom row: Panel 3 on the left, Panel 4 on the right. Keep all panels equal in size, with clear black borders, consistent spacing, and a balanced page layout.

Panel 1: Shot of the knight, chest held high, one hand resting on his sword hilt. He has a bold, determined expression and a confident smile. The king sits on his throne, listening. Speech bubble from the knight: "Your Majesty, I've come to slay the legendary White Dragon!"

Panel 2: Medium close-up of the king seated on his ornate throne. He looks at the knight with a neutral, composed expression, resting one hand on the armrest. The knight's shoulder is slightly visible in the foreground. Speech bubble from the king: "The dragon hasn't been seen for months."

Panel 3: Close-up of the knight's face as he tilts his head slightly and gives a self-assured smirk. His eyes show determination, and one hand rests casually on his sword hilt. Speech bubble from the knight: "That may be, but I'll still take a look."

Panel 4: Close-up of the king looking mildly annoyed, with slightly furrowed eyebrows and a subtle frown. He rests his cheek against one hand. The throne's ornate details frame him. Speech bubble from the king: "Good luck."
```

## Keeping Characters and Details Consistent Across Manga Panels

With the first page in hand, the next check is whether the knight and king still look like themselves from panel to panel. [Character consistency](https://blog.pixai.art/en/pixai-character-consistency-3-beginner-methods/) is essential when creating a manga page. Their poses, expressions, and camera angles should change, but their identities shouldn't.

For the most part, they held. The knight's face, hair, armor, and sword stayed recognizable across Panels 1 and 3, and the king's face and outfit carried over between Panels 2 and 4. The small differences we spotted came from the shift to close-ups and new angles, which is normal in manga and not a sign of the characters drifting.

The one real slip was the crown. The king wears it in Panel 2, but it's gone in Panel 4. Panel 1 also needed work, though for a different reason. The knight's design was right, but having him deliver his big line with his back to the king made the moment feel off.

We fixed both with targeted edits in Tsubaki.3, attaching the first page as the image to edit (that's what "@Reference_Image" refers to in the prompts below). Each fix took one or two attempts, and in both cases, mostly everything we asked to keep the same stayed the same.

## How to Fix Dialogue and Speech Bubbles in AI Manga Pages

Text was the trickiest part of our test, so we checked every speech bubble at full size instead of trusting how the page looked as a thumbnail. It helps to think of dialogue as three separate jobs. The model has to draw the bubble, place it next to the right speaker, and render the exact words you asked for.

Tsubaki.3 handled the first two well. Every bubble was clean and easy to read, each tail pointed to the right character, and none of them covered a face or key artwork. Panel 1's original line, the king's reply in Panel 2, and his "Good luck" in Panel 4 all came out exactly as written.

The wording is where things slipped. Panel 3 read "That may be, but I'll will take a look." And as we saw in the previous section, redrawing Panel 1 garbled the "e" in "Majesty." Editing a panel redraws its speech bubble too, so a line that was correct before can come back wrong.

We tried fixing both lines with a single targeted edit in Tsubaki.3.

```text
Edit the speech bubbles in panel 1 and panel 3 of @Reference_Image. In panel 1, 'Majesty' is not clear; refine the text. In panel 3, the text should be 'That may be, but I'll still take a look.' Keep everything else the same.
```

Panel 3 came back correct, but Panel 1 swapped one error for another. Instead of fixing the garbled letter, the model rewrote the word as "Your Magety." After a few more tries, "Majesty" still wasn't rendering correctly, so we changed the line instead of fighting the word. "My King" rendered cleanly, and the rest of the page stayed the same.

```text
Edit @Reference_Image. In panel 1, change the text in the speech bubble to 'My King, I've come to slay the legendary White Dragon!' Keep everything else the same.
```

## Common AI Manga Generator Issues and How We Fixed Them

Our test surfaced a few problems that are common when you use an AI manga panel generator to build a full page. Each one was fixable, but it needed a specific adjustment.

Here's a closer look at what went wrong and what helped:

- **Staging That Missed the Plan:** In Panel 1, the knight delivered his line with his back to the king, likely because the rewritten prompt placed the king in the background. A targeted Tsubaki.3 edit reframed the panel as a close-up, which fixed the staging in one or two attempts.
- **Disappearing Accessories:** The king's crown vanished in Panel 4, even though the rewritten prompt named it. Pointing the edit to the crown in Panel 2 restored it in one or two attempts, with the rest of the page untouched.
- **Text That Broke During Edits:** Panel 3 first read "I'll will," and redrawing Panel 1 garbled "Majesty," which a later fix attempt turned into "Magety." A targeted edit corrected Panel 3, but "Majesty" never rendered cleanly, so we reworded the line to "My King."
- **Untested Fallbacks:** If a word still won't render, PixAI Edit Pro or manual lettering in an image editor are other options, though we didn't try either in this test.

The smoothest edits were the ones in PixAI Studio, which changed only what we asked for and left every speech bubble intact. The practical lesson is to recheck the dialogue after any edit that redraws a panel with text, since that's where small errors tend to creep back in.

## Is Tsubaki.3 a Useful AI Manga Page Generator?

Tsubaki.3 is a useful AI manga generator, especially for short scenes. In our test, it kept both characters on model, placed every speech bubble next to the right speaker, and produced a page that reads clearly from start to finish. That makes it a good place to start if you're learning how to make manga with AI.

The first generation still needed some fixes. Panel 1 was staged awkwardly, the king's crown went missing in Panel 4, and one word never rendered correctly, so we reworded that line. Targeted edits handled the rest, and both of our PixAI Studio edits worked on the first attempt.

Before you share your own page, read every speech bubble at full size. You should also check that small accessories appear in every panel and that each character faces the person they're talking to. If you edit a panel with dialogue, recheck its text afterward.
